Reading the numbers

  • Cold and high-VDD corners are the hardest; hot corners are the easiest. Below 0 °C the median error is 2.0 % (bias +1.7 %), above 85 °C it is 1.2 %. When it is cold the sub-threshold slope is steepest, so log I is most sensitive to every input and a small error in any feature is amplified. At high VDD (above 1.07 V) DIBL is strongest, and VDD = 1.1 V is the edge of the training range. The worst gate samples (about 12 %) all sit at two corners that combine both: −35 °C at 1.08 V and −24 °C at 1.09 V.
  • A small leftover bias of about +2 % appears in INV 0, NAND2 01, NOR2 00 and OR2 11. Every one of them is dominated by the same device: an OFF NMOS with Vs = 0 and Vd ≈ VDD at W = 2L. This is the condition the effective-width fix (Chapter 6) improved from +3.6 %. The rest of that bias is close to the model's own transistor-level error (below 1 % median), but because every corner sees it in the same direction, it shows up as a bias and not as noise.
  • One isolated outlier (+126 %, 1 of 3,600). INV with input 1 at corner 198 (112 °C, 1.09 V). Here SPICE itself looks inconsistent: inside AND2, at the same corner, the same inverter state leaks 3.58 µA with its output at 42 mV, while the standalone inverter gives only 1.85 µA at 22 mV. The ML predicts 4.18 µA, closer to the AND2 value. We report it unchanged. Without it the maximum gate error is about 12 %.

Why the adder is less accurate than the gates

The decomposition in Chapter 8 answers this directly. Adding up SPICE gate leakages already misses the real adder by −2.6 % (median), with no ML involved. The ML part adds only about 1.3 % median error on top. The bias is negative because real logic levels are not ideal: a "0" sits a few to a hundred millivolts above ground and a "1" a little below VDD. Each such input turns the next gate's OFF transistor slightly on (exponentially, through VGS), so the real circuit leaks more than a table of ideal-input gates predicts.

The proof: flat ML

When the same transistor models are run on the adder's real node voltages ("flat ML"), the error drops to 1.2 % median with essentially zero bias. In version 2 this number was 5.2 %. The difference is the Vg-offset feature from Chapter 6: the v3 models can see a non-ideal input level and price it correctly. So the remaining adder error belongs to the ideal-level hierarchy, not to the ML, and a "levels-aware" hierarchy (next section) is the obvious next step.
